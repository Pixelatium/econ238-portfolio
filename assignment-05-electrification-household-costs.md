# Electrification Can Reduce Household Energy Costs

## A concise economic test of transportation, heating, water heating, and cooking in the United States

**Claim tested. Replacing direct fossil-fuel technologies with efficient electric alternatives can lower the cost of providing the same household services. The result is not automatic: it depends on technology efficiency, local energy prices, upfront costs, and the starting technology.**

**Bottom line. The strongest near-term operating-cost case is transportation and inefficient electric/propane/oil heating. Heat pumps can also compete with natural gas, but the advantage is more location-sensitive. Electrification works economically because EVs, heat pumps, heat-pump water heaters, and induction equipment often require less purchased energy for the same service.**

*Figure 2. Recreated from a DOE Better Buildings presentation citing the Electrification Futures Study. Delivered heating cost: electric resistance $42.30/MMBtu, air-source heat pump $18.00, cold-climate heat pump $21.40, reference gas furnace $22.20. [4]*

**The comparison shows why electrification should not be modeled as simply replacing one unit of gas energy with one unit of electricity. Heat pumps move heat rather than create it. DOE states that modern heat pumps can reduce heating electricity use by about 65% relative to electric resistance heating. [1]**

**This does not imply every gas-heated household saves money. DOE's more recent national analysis finds that savings depend on the existing fuel, climate, equipment, and utility rates; savings are generally strongest for homes switching from propane, heating oil, or older electric equipment, while some natural-gas homes also save. [5]**

## 2. Water heating and cooking

**ENERGY STAR reports that certified heat-pump water heaters use about 70% less energy than standard electric water heaters and can save a four-person household about $550 per year under its stated assumptions. [2] DOE also reports that induction cooking can be up to three times as efficient as gas cooking. [3]**

## 3. Transportation: a clear operating-cost case

**For a transparent national benchmark, the calculation below assumes 12,000 miles/year, a gasoline vehicle at 25.6 mpg, a typical EV at 39 kWh/100 miles, 2025 gasoline at $3.097/gallon, and 2025 residential electricity at $0.173/kWh. EPA supplies the vehicle-efficiency values; EIA supplies energy prices. [6,8-10]**

![Figure 4. Recreated calculation. Gasoline: 12,000/25.6 × $3.097 = $1,452/yr. EV: 12,000/100 × 39 × $0.173 = $810/yr. DOE AFDC maintenance estimates add about $1,212/yr for gasoline versus $732/yr for an EV. [8-11]]({{ '/assets/images/electrification-fig4.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 4. Recreated calculation. Gasoline: 12,000/25.6 × $3.097 = $1,452/yr. EV: 12,000/100 × 39 × $0.173 = $810/yr. DOE AFDC maintenance estimates add about $1,212/yr for gasoline versus $732/yr for an EV. [8-11]*

**Under these assumptions, modeled annual fuel + maintenance cost is about $2,664 for the gasoline vehicle versus $1,542 for the EV, a difference of roughly $1,122 per year. This is an operating-cost comparison only; purchase price, financing, insurance, charging installation, and resale value are excluded.**

## 4. Economic conclusion

Service Economic result Main condition

Space heating Often favorable for heat pumps Climate + gas/electric rates matter

Water heating Strong vs. resistance electric Higher upfront equipment cost

Cooking Efficiency gain; small total bill share Appliance + electrical upgrade cost

Transportation Large operating-cost advantage in benchmark Vehicle price + charging access matter

**Defensible argument for debate: Household electrification can reduce operating costs without reducing household services because the best electric technologies convert purchased energy into useful services more efficiently than combustion technologies. However, the policy case should not claim universal savings. The strongest argument is for targeted electrification where efficiency gains exceed the electricity-price and capital-cost penalty, rather than a blanket assumption that every household benefits.**

**What could overturn the result? Very high electricity prices, very low natural-gas prices, mild usage that produces little annual savings, expensive panel/wiring upgrades, or high incremental purchase prices can lengthen or eliminate payback. Conversely, replacement at end-of-life, managed EV charging, and falling equipment costs improve the economics.**
