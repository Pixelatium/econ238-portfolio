# ENERGY-24: How Extraordinary Is Current Electricity-Demand Growth?

## A public-facing visual brief on the U.S. electricity-demand comeback

**One-sentence answer:** Today's growth is not historically unprecedented. What is unusual is that demand is accelerating after about two decades of near-flat growth while utilities are repeatedly raising their forecasts and racing to build supply.

**Why the public should care: Electricity demand affects power-plant construction, transmission lines, utility investment, reliability, and ultimately electricity prices. A forecast that changes quickly is harder and more expensive to plan around than a stable forecast.**

## What changed?

**For years, U.S. electricity demand barely moved. Now data centers, new manufacturing, electrification, and other large loads are pushing demand upward again. EIA's September 2026 outlook expects U.S. electricity sales to rise to 4,135 billion kWh in 2026 and 4,211 billion kWh in 2027, with commercial demand playing the largest role in the increase. [3]**

![Figure 3. NERC's successive assessments increased projected 10-year summer peak-demand growth from 55 GW to 224 GW. [6-7]]({{ '/assets/images/energy24-fig3.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 3. NERC's successive assessments increased projected 10-year summer peak-demand growth from 55 GW to 224 GW. [6-7]*

**Simple interpretation: The forecast is moving almost as dramatically as demand itself. A power plant or transmission line can take years to permit, finance, connect, and build. If planners discover the need late, the system has less time to respond.**

![Figure 4. NERC projects 224 GW of summer peak-demand growth over ten years. It identifies 71 GW of approved supply additions and 266 GW of prospective additions. Prospective projects are a pipeline, not guaranteed completed capacity. [6]]({{ '/assets/images/energy24-fig4.png' | relative_url }})

## Is this really unprecedented?

**No. The United States has experienced much faster growth before. Electricity demand grew around 7.3% per year in the 1960s and 4.7% in the 1970s. The difference is that the power sector was then accustomed to continuous expansion. Today's industry is pivoting out of a long period in which demand growth was weak. [1-2]**

![Figure 5. EIA data show net generation rising from 759.2 billion kWh in 1960 to 1,535.1 billion kWh in 1970 - about a 7.3% compound annual increase. [8]]({{ '/assets/images/energy24-fig5.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 5. EIA data show net generation rising from 759.2 billion kWh in 1960 to 1,535.1 billion kWh in 1970 - about a 7.3% compound annual increase. [8]*

**The useful historical lesson: Fast supply expansion is possible. The question is whether today's permitting, transmission, interconnection, equipment supply chains, and investment process can move quickly enough after years of relatively little demand growth.**

## The argument in one picture

## What people hear What the data show

## "Demand growth is unprecedented." No. The 1960s and 1970s grew much faster.

## "The grid cannot add enough power." History shows very rapid expansion is possible.

## "There is no supply coming." A large prospective pipeline exists, but much is not yet approved.

## "The main problem is demand itself." The bigger risk is the speed mismatch between new load and infrastructure.

**Debate position: Current U.S. electricity-demand growth is extraordinary mainly as a planning shock, not as a historical growth rate. America has managed much faster growth before. The economic challenge today is that large new loads are appearing after a long flat-demand era, while forecasts keep rising faster than firmly approved generation and grid infrastructure. The best policy response is therefore to speed up infrastructure adjustment - generation, transmission, interconnection, permitting, and equipment supply - rather than treating demand growth itself as an unprecedented crisis.**
