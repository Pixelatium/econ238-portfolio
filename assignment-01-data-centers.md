News articles flashing headlines like “AI data center will use 800,000 gallons of water a day” or it requires electricity to “power an entire city” can be scary. Let's not get deceived by the numbers. A typical university and a major hospital system consumes just as much water and electricity, and you don’t see anyone complaining or protesting about banning hospitals or schools. It’s not about how much resources they consume, it is about what sort of benefit we get in return.

Let’s think economically and try to assess the situation like a professional. We don’t feel awful about building new schools and hospitals. We graciously accept the necessary resource consumption, because both foundations benefit the local community greatly. University of Rochester has around 1000 permanent faculties, ([Department of Computer Science : University of Rochester](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/)), and major hospitals like Mayo Clinic or Cleveland Clinic hires over 4000 people. So these organizations create jobs, potential customers for the local community, and most importantly, they offer an essential value to the community: education and health.

As for the modern data center, they only hire 20~50 employees, ([Department of Computer Science : University of Rochester](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/)), and do not benefit the local community at all. You have access to the data center with your PC irrelevant to the location of the data center. To think of all the heat it would generate, all the environment that it will damage, it is questionable why any local community would want this thing around.

The most important question to ask here is who pays the bill. Building the data center will require much more electricity than what the city needed before, and electricity companies would need to expand their power lines, which will add on to everyday joe’s electricity bill for this month. Local governments offering tax cuts means that it adds more burden to the citizens. In short: Big Tech makes billions, while local residents get higher utility bills, strained water tables, and almost no jobs.

I’m not saying that we need to ban the technology completely. But the rules that we are playing by right now, does not seem fair at all. Cities must stop giving tax breaks to facilities that barely hire anyone, and regulators must force tech companies to pay the full price for their own power grid upgrades and water-recycling systems. Once tech giants are forced to pay their own way instead of pushing costs onto our wallets, those scary headlines will lose their power.
