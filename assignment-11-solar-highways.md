# SOLAR HIGHWAYS

Turning America’s Roads into Power Plants

ECON 238 | Week Eleven: Batsh!t Environmental Idea | Fall 2026

Central question: Can an elevated solar roof over a highway generate enough additional value to justify

its high structural cost, safety obligations, and traffic disruption?

## 1. The proposal and the unusual idea

I propose building modular solar-panel roofs over selected sections of existing U.S. highways. Cars would

travel below the roof while photovoltaic panels produce power above it. The electricity could be sold to

the grid or delivered to highway rest areas, charging stations, and nearby facilities. Unlike solar

pavement, panels would be mounted above traffic, away from tire wear. The unusual part is treating

transportation corridors as dual-purpose energy infrastructure rather than acquiring a separate parcel of

land.

This is not a proposal to cover every highway immediately. The first stage is a one-kilometer pilot with a

12-meter-wide canopy. Its success should be judged against conventional solar projects, not against

doing nothing. I would only support expansion when measured benefits exceed the extra costs and risks.

## 2. Why the idea matters—and why it sounds crazy

Solar power can reduce emissions from electricity generation. Highway rights-of-way already connect

population centers and consume extensive land. Reusing part of this footprint might be attractive where

land acquisition is expensive or new solar projects face local opposition. A canopy could also shade the

road and shelter selected equipment, although road-maintenance savings must be demonstrated rather

than assumed.

The challenge is that a highway roof must survive wind, snow, vibration, crashes, drainage problems,

fires, and decades of exposure. Installing or repairing it may close traffic lanes. A falling component could

cause serious harm. Ordinary ground-mounted solar avoids many of these burdens. This is why a

technically possible project may still be economically unwise.

## 3. Site choice and engineering boundaries

I would begin with a straight, sunny segment near a substation, away from ramps, bridges, tunnels,

complex intersections, and areas with high wind or heavy snow. A state transportation agency would

need to approve clearances, structural foundations, crash barriers, emergency access, fire response,

inspection plans, and safe lane-closure schedules. This classroom model does not establish engineering

feasibility or permission to build.

## 4. The full back-of-the-envelope calculation

Step 1: roof area. 1,000 m × 12 m = 12,000 m².

Step 2: panel area. 12,000 m² × 0.70 = 8,400 m².

Step 3: solar capacity. 8,400 m² × 0.20 kW/m² = 1,680 kW = 1.68 MW.

Step 4: annual electricity. 1,680 kW × 1,400 kWh/kW-year = 2,352,000 kWh = 2.352 GWh/year. This

assumes a full representative year, not constant 24-hour production.

Step 5: annual electricity value. 2,352,000 kWh × $0.10/kWh = $235,200/year. This is gross modeled

value, not guaranteed cash received by a developer.

Step 6: capital cost. Structure: 12,000 m² × $450/m² = $5,400,000. Solar equipment: 1,680 kW ×

$1,200/kW = $2,016,000. Total = $7,416,000.

Step 7: operating costs and simple payback. O&M;: 0.015 × $7,416,000 = $111,240/year. Annual net

operating cash before financing and tax: $235,200 − $111,240 = $123,960. Simple payback: $7,416,000

/ $123,960 = 59.8 years.

## 5. What does the financial result actually mean?

A nearly 60-year simple payback is a serious warning. It does not account for interest, panel degradation,

inverter replacement, major repairs, decommissioning, or the time value of money. It should not be

described as an attractive investment. The elevated structure accounts for $5.4 million of the $7.416

million capital cost, or about 73%. The expensive roof—not the solar modules—is the dominant obstacle.

Discounted cash flow: a more demanding test

For a deliberately simplified 25-year project, assume a constant $123,960 annual operating surplus and a

5% real discount rate, with no terminal value or replacement costs. The annuity factor is [1 −

(1.05)^(-25)] / 0.05 = 14.094. Present value of operating surplus = $123,960 × 14.094 ≈ $1.747 million.

Net present value (NPV) ≈ −$5.669 million. This is a classroom scenario, not a bankable financial model.

2

The electricity price needed for zero NPV under these same assumptions is approximately ($111,240 +

$7,416,000 / 14.094) / 2,352,000 = $0.271/kWh, far above the modeled $0.10/kWh. Additional benefits,

subsidies, lower construction costs, or much higher electricity value would be necessary.

## 6. Sensitivity: when could the idea become less unreasonable?

Holding electricity production at 2.352 GWh/year and O&M; at 1.5% of total upfront cost, changes in the

structure price dramatically alter simple payback:

Structure cost / m² Upfront cost Annual surplus Simple payback

$300 $5.616m $150,960 37.2 years

$450 (base) $7.416m $123,960 59.8 years

$650 $9.816m $87,960 111.6 years

At the base $450/m² structure cost, electricity valued at $0.08/kWh yields $76,920/year in net operating

cash and about 96.4 years of simple payback. At $0.12/kWh, it yields $171,000/year and about 43.4

years. This is why the pilot should prioritize locations where land scarcity, high electricity value, or

verifiable co-benefits are unusually large.

Scaling the thought experiment

If 100 similar kilometers could be built with the same productivity and cost, the program would have 168

MW of capacity, produce 235.2 GWh/year, and require $741.6 million upfront. This linear scaling ignores

site differences, transmission upgrades, material bottlenecks, permitting, and economies or

diseconomies of scale. Scaling a bad payback does not automatically make it good.

## 7. Environmental accounting: benefits and hidden costs

Using an assumed displaced-grid emissions rate of 0.35 kg CO2/kWh, avoided operating emissions are

2,352,000 × 0.35 = 823,200 kg CO2, or 823 metric tons per year. At a hypothetical $50 per ton of CO2,

this would be worth roughly $41,160/year as a social climate benefit. That is not necessarily revenue to

the project owner and must not be double-counted if the electricity price already incorporates carbon

costs.

The gross figure is not net lifecycle emissions. Steel, concrete, panel manufacturing, construction traffic,

maintenance, and disposal create emissions. A full study would subtract those impacts and compare the

canopy with ground solar producing the same electricity. The canopy could also change runoff, road

lighting, snow behavior, wildlife movement, and local visual conditions.

## 8. Economics: incentives, externalities, and opportunity cost

Opportunity cost and the proper counterfactual

The relevant question is whether $7.416 million should fund this canopy rather than a ground-mounted

solar array, grid improvements, transit, road maintenance, or another project. The fact that the roof

produces clean power does not prove that it is the best use of public money. A pilot should publish the

cost per delivered kWh and compare it with a conventional solar project at the same site or nearby.

3

Externalities and who bears them

Cleaner electricity may benefit people who never buy the power through lower air pollution and

greenhouse-gas emissions. On the other side, motorists bear traffic delays, nearby residents may bear

construction noise, and the public bears some safety and emergency-response risk. These external costs

must be included in the social cost-benefit calculation, even when they are absent from a developer’s

budget.

Property rights, contracts, and incentives

State agencies control the highway corridor, utilities control interconnection, and private developers may

be best positioned to build and operate solar facilities. A concession could grant a developer permission

to use the overhead space in exchange for electricity revenue and strict maintenance obligations. The

contract should specify who pays for crashes, repairs, closures, equipment removal, insurance, and

end-of-life restoration. Without clear liability, firms may shift costs onto taxpayers.

Political economy and distribution

Drivers may oppose construction delays even if society benefits from cleaner power. Utilities may

welcome generation or resist expensive interconnections. Construction firms may benefit from contracts,

while residents may worry about aesthetics and noise. Low-income communities should not receive the

traffic disruption while distant investors receive all the revenue. Site selection and compensation should

be transparent.

## 9. Why not already build these everywhere?

The current approach is to place solar panels on roofs, parking canopies, or ground sites where

construction and maintenance are simpler. These methods are established because they avoid the extra

requirement of safely suspending a large structure over fast-moving vehicles. Highway solar is not

necessarily missing because policymakers lack imagination. It may be missing because the added

engineering burden has a weak economic return.

There is a real Chesterton Fence problem: roads are open to the sky partly because emergency access,

clearance, visibility, maintenance, and weather management are easier. We should understand these

functions before altering them. A canopy pilot must preserve or replace them, not simply assume they

are unimportant.

## 10. Main objections and possible responses

Objection 1 — catastrophic safety risk. A component falling onto moving traffic could be disastrous.

Response: require independent structural review, crash protection, fire and emergency plans, routine

inspections, and conservative design standards. If safe operation is not demonstrated, the project should

stop.

Objection 2 — cost and road closures. The roof may cost much more than the electricity is worth.

Response: build during planned reconstruction, standardize modules, avoid complicated sites, and

require competitive bids. These steps might reduce costs but cannot be assumed to solve the financial

problem.

4

Objection 3 — unequal benefits. The community may bear noise and traffic delays while a private

developer collects revenue. Response: publish the distribution of costs, include community-benefit

terms, and compensate measurable harms.

Objection 4 — unintended environmental effects. Added steel and concrete may undermine the

emissions case; shade may change pavement performance; improved road comfort might encourage

extra driving. Response: conduct lifecycle emissions analysis and monitor traffic, runoff, lighting, and

maintenance before scaling.

## 11. A practical experiment with a clear stop rule

Phase 1: feasibility. Screen possible corridors for sunlight, utility access, lane geometry, safety, and

environmental justice. Obtain conceptual engineering review and preliminary cost estimates. Compare

each candidate against ground solar.

Phase 2: competitive pilot. Select one suitable kilometer or a shorter test span if engineering requires it.

Publish the budget and baseline assumptions before construction. Collect hourly generation, downtime,

lane-closure hours, maintenance spending, and safety incidents.

Phase 3: three-year review. Report actual kWh, total cost, traffic delay, lifecycle emissions, and updated

NPV. Compare measured performance against an alternative solar project. Expand only if the canopy’s

added benefits justify its extra cost under transparent assumptions. Otherwise, publish the failure and

move on.

## 12. Conclusion: the lesson from the numbers

I like the idea of making roads do two jobs at once. A highway that generates electricity is memorable

and could be useful in rare places where land is scarce and electricity is valuable. But my own base-case

numbers do not support covering highways at scale. The project is more convincing as a carefully

controlled experiment than as a nationwide mandate. Its value is not only the electricity it might

produce. It also forces us to ask when creative reuse of public infrastructure creates real social

value—and when a dramatic idea is simply too expensive.
